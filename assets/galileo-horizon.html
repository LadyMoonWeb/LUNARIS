Galileo Horizon

The horizon of a south-polar landing site, drawn in the manner of Galileo's 1610 ink-wash Moon and engraved star atlases. The Sun crawls along the horizon and hides behind ridges; the Earth bobs with libration and changes phase.

Why this style

Galileo was the first to see that the Moon has mountains, from the shadows along the terminator. This tool does the same with the LOLA laser altimeter. The plate links 1610 and 2026.

What is physical here

Ridges opposite the Sun are lit; ridges on the Sun's side are dark silhouettes. The Earth's phase is opposite: when the Sun stands near the Earth in the sky, the Earth is nearly "new". When terrain hides the Sun, the sky darkens: no power. The Sun disc is drawn larger than its true 0.53°, and the vertical scale is stretched.

Data

The silhouette comes from setHorizonMask(array): 720 horizon elevations in degrees, azimuth 0–359.5° in 0.5° steps. Sun and Earth tracks come from setTracks({sun, earth}): lists of {t, az, alt} from the app's ephemeris. Without tracks the plate shows an illustrative lunar day.
